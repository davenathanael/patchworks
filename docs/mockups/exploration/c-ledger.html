Candidate C — Ledger

The reconciliation of "airy and calm" with "dense and utilitarian." Information density comes from structure — real aligned columns, comparable numbers, hairline rules — rather than from cramming. Texture comes from typography: body and content in a serif at generous measure, chrome and metadata in sans and mono at small sizes. Navigation is a thin rail of labels with counts, more like a table of contents than a menu. The result is a surface that shows you everything and asks for nothing.

The bet: density and calm aren't opposites. A printed ledger, a well-set contents page, a Bloomberg terminal in a serif — all dense, none of them loud. What makes noise is variation, not quantity.

1 · Bookmark list

The list is the ledger: title, host, tags, saved date, state. Because the columns are aligned you can scan a whole page vertically down the host or tag column and actually see patterns — which host you save from most, which tag you've abandoned. That's information the card layouts cannot give you at all. Unread rows carry a warm background tint and a 2px edge; queued items get a green dot in the state column.

Postgres VACUUM, finally explained properly

brandur.orgqueued 1 week agoopen original ↗

Vacuum is the most misunderstood routine in PostgreSQL, and most of the confusion comes from documentation that describes the mechanism accurately but the purpose poorly. The purpose is simple: reclaim space and keep the visibility map honest.

Vacuum exists to answer two questions: where is the dead space, and who is allowed to see which row version.

Everything downstream — index bloat, autovacuum thresholds, the aggressive-worker heuristics — is a consequence of those two questions being expensive to answer.

What this direction is good and bad for

Good for — organize and retrieve. Because every list is the same component with the same columns, "where do I look for X" becomes a learnable reflex instead of a per-screen hunt. Vertical scanning down the host or tags column surfaces patterns — your most-saved sources, your abandoned tags — that no card layout reveals. Collections is the strongest collections view in the set, because role and shared state are just columns. And it keeps the serif display face you currently like, in a place where it does real work.

Bad for — mobile. Three columns is the ceiling, and past that this degrades rather than adapts. Actions are hover-revealed on desktop (always visible on mobile, which is the right call, but costs height). Notes-as-sub-line is a nice compromise that doesn't scale to long notes. And the thin label rail, while distinctive, is a slightly unusual navigation idiom — it's a table of contents, and it assumes you'll learn it once rather than scanning it every time.

The open question if you pick this direction: does the serif-body / sans-chrome split hold up across every screen, or does it start feeling like two designs? It's the strongest typographic argument in the set and also the one with the most ways to go wrong.
