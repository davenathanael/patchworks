Candidate B — Inbox

The calm extreme. One centered column, no persistent navigation, no chrome competing with the content. Bookmarks are cards with enough presence to feel like things rather than rows. Quick-add is a floating composer pinned to the bottom — the pattern your thumb already knows from every phone you own — and on mobile it's the only persistent control. Type is larger, corners are softer, and the whole thing is designed to be looked at rather than operated.

The bet: your most common act is reading and retrieving, not operating. A surface can be calm and fast if it never asks you to look at machinery. The cost is real: fewer rows per screen, more scrolling, and less at-a-glance comparison than a table.

Postgres VACUUM, finally explained properly

brandur.org · queued 1 week ago · open original ↗

Vacuum is the most misunderstood routine in PostgreSQL, and most of the confusion comes from documentation that describes the mechanism accurately but the purpose poorly. The purpose is simple: reclaim space and keep the visibility map honest.

Vacuum exists to answer two questions: where is the dead space, and who is allowed to see which row version.

Everything downstream — index bloat, autovacuum thresholds, the aggressive-worker heuristics — is a consequence of those two questions being expensive to answer.

The multixact section is the one I need for the pagination work. Everything before it is a rehash of the official docs.

What this direction is good and bad for

Good for — reading and mobile. Notes are the best-supported element in the whole mockup, which matters if you're annotate-then-forget. The card treatment makes triage feel unhurried in a way that a table never will. Mobile is a first-class citizen here rather than a fallback: the bottom composer and bottom nav are patterns you already have motor memory for, so the app feels native immediately.

Bad for — retrieval and organization at scale. Nine rows per screen means scrolling through 142 bookmarks. You can't compare a host or a date across items without reading each one, and card-height variance means the eye has no consistent rail to follow. For organize-heavy work — comparing collection counts, sorting by last-modified, checking what's unread where — a grid of cards is the wrong instrument. Sharing a link to the app on a phone also feels heavier than it should, because cards don't compress.

The open question if you pick this direction: can the card get denser without collapsing into the current row-based design? A "compact card" is a real thing and might be the compromise, but it's also just a row with padding.
