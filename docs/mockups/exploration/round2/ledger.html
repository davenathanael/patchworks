Candidate 2 — Ledger

Round 1's C, taken seriously as a mobile structure rather than a desktop one. The column alignment is real and it's what makes this direction worth choosing — you can scan straight down the host or tag column and see which sources dominate. But round 1 treated that as a desktop-only idea and stubbed mobile. Here the mobile presentation is the primary design and the columns are simply what appears when there's room for them. Same data, same components, one layout that earns its density as width allows.

Two round 1 mistakes corrected: the note is no longer a 0-or-1 count column but always-visible text (point 7), and the rail's navigation labels change to match the action vocabulary of wherever you are — selecting a queue shows Done / Later, not Read / Queue (point 6).

Depth options

The paper option is strongest here — the ledger is already a printed-document metaphor, so a slight lift reads as intentional rather than decorative.

1 · Fully flat

The ledger already reads as flat and printed, so this costs the least here. Buttons lose their press affordance without a shadow.

2 · Default — borders + button shadow

Keeps the paper feel while still giving controls tactility. Safe default.

3 · Paper — layered

Fits the metaphor best of the three candidates — cards and rows lift slightly, controls feel raised. Risk is low here precisely because the design is already document-like.

Wide-screen options

This IA has the strongest wide-screen case of the three, because a ledger is about columns and more width means more columns — host, saved, state, and eventually reading time.

Verdict on this direction

Strongest at — retrieval and organization, by a clear margin. This is the only candidate where you can scan 20 items and answer "how much of this is from brandur.org" without reading a single title. Collections is the best collections view in the set, because role, shared state, and counts are columns. And it's the only one that naturally uses extra width — a wide screen adds columns, which is more information rather than more whitespace.

Still weak at — notes, which is the thing candidate 1 is built around. A note here is a clamped single line on desktop, which is exactly the compromise round 1 objected to. If your notes are long and you actually reread them, this IA will keep disappointing you. Mobile is workable but not distinctive: it's the same list with fewer columns, which is correct but not memorable.

The tension this candidate can't resolve: columns are what make it strong and what force notes to compress. If note-reading matters more than column-scanning, candidate 1 wins; if the reverse, this one does.
