Candidate 3 — Split Sheet

Round 1's A with the desktop framing removed. The inspector idea survives because it's genuinely good — you can edit notes and tags without ever leaving the list — but it is no longer a two-pane layout. On mobile it is a bottom sheet that slides up over the list; the side pane only appears past 54rem, as a bonus rather than the premise. Rows stay dense, actions stay permanently visible, and the keyboard remains the fast path. This is the least approachable of the three and the most efficient once you learn it.

The trade to watch: because actions live on the selected item's sheet rather than on the row, this IA makes discovery worse than candidates 1 and 2. The rows keep a small always-visible action set as a compensation, but the full set is one tap away in the sheet.

1 · Bookmark list

Mobile: tap a row, the sheet rises with the full record — note editable, tags as toggles, everything addressable. Desktop: same content, docked right. Number keys and j/k move selection; the sheet follows.

Depth options

This is the one candidate where paper-layering is most dangerous — a dense list plus raised rows plus a floating sheet is a lot of depth competing at once. The flat option is arguably its best.

1 · Fully flat — arguably best here
+

A dense list with 60 visible elements per screen cannot afford elevation. Depth here competes with the data rather than organising it.

2 · Default — borders + button shadow
+

Works because the depth is confined to buttons. The sheet still gets a real shadow on mobile since it genuinely floats over content.

3 · Paper — layered
+

Rows lift slightly, which gives the selected row real presence — but at 60 rows per screen the repeated shadows create a texture you didn't ask for. Right answer is probably option 1 or 2.

Verdict on this direction

Strongest at — editing and bulk work, by a wide margin. The sheet is a real editor: the note is a textarea, tags and collections are toggles, and nothing requires leaving the list. Selection-driven triage means 50 items is 50 keystrokes. This is the only candidate where round 1's "engineer appeal" survives without the desktop-only framing that killed it.

Still weak at — discovery and calm. Actions are behind selection, so a first-time visitor has no idea what's possible; the j/k/s/r model is invisible unless you look for it. The dense rows carry less note text than candidate 1 and less cross-item scanning than candidate 2. It's also the least mobile-native — a bottom sheet is a good pattern, but it's still a pattern borrowed from desktop.

The honest framing: this is the candidate to pick if you're confident you'll keep using this daily. If there's any chance it becomes a casual browser you visit weekly, candidate 1 is the safer bet and this one will feel like operating a tool you never learned.
